Chapter 0 · Warm-up

Strands, direction & the RNA product

You should be able to
  • Name both strands of a gene (coding/sense/+ vs template/antisense/–).
  • Write the RNA transcript 5′→3′ from either strand, however it is written.
Key points
  • RNA is synthesised 5′→3′. The DNA strand that is read (antiparallel, 3′→5′) is the template strand.
  • The other strand has the same sequence as the RNA (T instead of U). It is called the coding / sense / plus (+) / non-template strand. Databases list this strand.
  • The template strand is also called the non-coding / antisense / minus (–) strand.
  • For a single gene only one strand is used as the template. Different genes on the same chromosome can use different strands.

Chapter 1

The transcription unit

A bacterial gene has a promoter, an RNA-coding sequence and a terminator.

You should be able to
  • Name the three parts of a transcription unit and what each does.
  • Explain how the promoter sets the direction of transcription and which strand is the template.
  • List the substrates of RNA polymerase.
Key points
  • DNA-dependent RNA polymerase catalyses transcription and makes RNA 5′→3′.
  • Substrates are the ribonucleoside triphosphates ATP, GTP, CTP, UTP. The next nucleotide is chosen by base-pairing with the exposed template base, and pyrophosphate is released.
  • RNA polymerase needs no primer. Unlike DNA polymerase, it can start a new chain on its own.
  • Promoter: lies upstream; RNA polymerase binds here. Its orientation tells the enzyme which strand is the template and where to start, so every RNA begins at the same site (+1).
  • RNA-coding sequence: the stretch of DNA that is copied into RNA.
  • Terminator: specifies where transcription stops.
  • A gene with its promoter is an independent unit. Some genes use one strand as the template; others use the opposite strand.

Chapter 2

The promoter

Short DNA elements tell RNA polymerase where to bind and how often to start. How closely they match the consensus sets the promoter's strength.

You should be able to
  • Give the consensus sequences and positions of the –35 and –10 boxes.
  • Describe the UP element, the extended –10 element and the discriminator.
  • Predict the relative strength of promoters from their sequences.
Key points
  • Most E. coli promoters have two key elements, about 35 and 10 bp upstream of +1 (the first transcribed base pair).
  • –35 box consensus: 5′-TTGACA-3′. –10 box (formerly the Pribnow box): 5′-TATAAT-3′. They are usually separated by about 17 bp.
  • A consensus is derived by comparing many promoters. Very few match it exactly; most differ at a few positions.
  • Promoter strength = the number of transcripts initiated per unit time. It depends on (i) initial binding of the polymerase, (ii) how efficiently isomerization occurs and (iii) how readily the polymerase escapes.
  • Promoters closer to the consensus are generally stronger. For example, a –10 box of GATACT initiates less often than TATAAT because σ recognises it less well. Genes needed at high levels tend to have near-consensus promoters, which is why promoter sequences vary so much.
  • UP element: an extra AT-rich element upstream of –35 in some strong promoters (e.g. rRNA genes). It provides an additional specific contact with the polymerase, so binding is stronger.
  • Extended –10: some promoters lack a –35 region and instead have a short extra element at the upstream end of the –10 box (e.g. the gal genes). The extra contacts compensate for the missing –35.
  • Discriminator: an element just downstream of –10. How strongly it interacts with the polymerase affects the stability of the enzyme–promoter complex.

Chapter 3

RNA polymerase & σ factors

One enzyme, many σ factors. The core enzyme makes RNA, and σ tells it where to start.

You should be able to
  • Give the subunit composition of core enzyme and holoenzyme, with genes and functions.
  • Explain why σ is needed for initiation but not for elongation.
  • Map the σ70 regions (and αCTD) to the promoter elements they recognise.
  • Match alternative σ factors to the conditions in which they act.
Key points
  • Bacteria have only one type of RNA polymerase. It transcribes all classes of gene: mRNA, tRNA and rRNA.
  • Core enzyme = α2ββ′ω. Adding σ (the initiation factor) gives the holoenzyme.
  • Without σ, the core enzyme binds anywhere on the DNA and starts at incorrect sites. σ greatly reduces non-specific binding and gives the enzyme its promoter-specific binding. σ is not needed for elongation or termination.
  • σ70 has four regions. Region 4 recognises –35 using a helix-turn-helix: one helix sits in the major groove and contacts the bases, while the other lies across the groove and contacts the backbone. Region 2 recognises –10 with an α-helix whose aromatic residues bind non-template bases and stabilise the melted DNA. Region 2.3 melts the DNA.
  • Region 3 recognises the extended –10 (two specific base pairs). Region 1.2 recognises the discriminator.
  • The UP element is recognised not by σ but by the α C-terminal domain (αCTD). The αCTD is joined to αNTD by a flexible linker, so it can reach UP elements that lie further upstream.
  • Different σ factors direct the core enzyme to different sets of promoters, for example σ32 during heat shock (its promoter elements lie at about –39 and –15).

Chapter 4

Initiation

Initiation goes from a closed complex to an open complex, then through abortive cycles and scrunching, and ends when the enzyme escapes the promoter.

You should be able to
  • Describe the closed and open complexes, and isomerization, with the positions and footprint sizes.
  • Explain why abortive initiation happens and how scrunching powers promoter escape.
  • Name the five channels of RNA polymerase.
Key points
  • The holoenzyme first contacts –35, then binds the whole promoter with the DNA still double-stranded. This is the closed complex, and its formation is readily reversible.
  • Isomerization converts it to the open complex: the DNA melts from –11 to +2 (the transcription bubble, about 12–14 bp). With σ70 this needs no ATP; it is a spontaneous conformational change to a more favourable form. It is essentially irreversible and normally guarantees that initiation follows.
  • In the –10 box, A–11 and T–7 of the non-template strand flip out of the base stack into pockets in σ. This stabilises the single-stranded form and drives melting.
  • In the open complex the polymerase contacts about 75 bp (–55 to +20).
  • The first two NTPs are aligned on the template and joined, then more are added. This is the initial transcribing complex. Adding the first ~10 nt is inefficient, and short RNAs (<10 nt) are repeatedly released: abortive synthesis.
  • Proposed reason: the σ region 3/4 linker mimics RNA and sits in the RNA exit channel. It must be ejected before the RNA can grow beyond about 10 nt, and this can take several attempts.
  • Scrunching (the accepted model): the enzyme stays put and pulls downstream DNA into itself, so the bubble grows to 22–24 bp. This stores energy, which is used to eject the 3/4 linker and break the polymerase–promoter and core–σ contacts.
  • Once the transcript is longer than about 10 nt the enzyme has escaped the promoter. Scrunching reverses, the bubble collapses back to 12–14 bp, and a stable ternary complex (enzyme + DNA + RNA) begins elongation. σ is weakened or lost.

Chapter 5

Elongation

The core enzyme moves along the gene with a bubble of constant size, proofreads its work, and is rescued when it stalls.

You should be able to
  • Give the size of the bubble, the RNA–DNA hybrid, the footprint and the rate of elongation.
  • Distinguish pyrophosphorolytic editing from hydrolytic editing.
  • Explain transcription-coupled repair by TRCF.
Key points
  • Once the transcript is about 10 nt or longer it no longer fits in the hybrid region and threads into the RNA exit channel. All contacts with the promoter and its regulators are broken.
  • σ is released after about 10 nt, and the core enzyme alone completes the gene. The enzyme becomes more compact and contacts about 40 bp of DNA, with a bubble of 12–14 bp that stays the same size throughout elongation.
  • Within the bubble about 9 nt of RNA is base-paired to the template (the RNA–DNA hybrid). The rest leaves the enzyme as a single strand, and the DNA strands re-anneal behind the enzyme.
  • The rate is about 40 nt/s at 37 °C. Because RNA is displaced just behind the active site, many polymerases can transcribe the same gene at once.
  • Error rate: about 1 in 104, compared with about 1 in 107 for replication, because transcription has less proofreading.
  • Proofreading: pyrophosphorolytic editing (the enzyme reverses the synthesis reaction, removing the wrong nucleotide, then re-adds the correct one) and hydrolytic editing (the enzyme backs up one or more nt and cleaves the RNA, then resumes).
  • When the polymerase is arrested (e.g. at DNA damage), TRCF (an ATPase) binds DNA upstream, translocates to the stalled polymerase and pushes it. The polymerase either restarts or, more often, the ternary complex dissociates. TRCF then recruits UvrA(B)C: transcription-coupled repair.

Chapter 6

Termination

Transcription stops at one of two kinds of terminator, intrinsic or Rho-dependent. (Removal of a stalled enzyme by TRCF is a special case.)

You should be able to
  • Describe a Rho-independent terminator and explain why both the hairpin and the U-tract are required.
  • Describe the rut site and the structure and action of Rho.
  • Predict the effect of mutations on termination.
Key points
  • Rho-independent terminators (type I, intrinsic) are used by the core enzyme alone. Rho-dependent terminators (type II) need the protein Rho (ρ). A given gene has one type or the other.
  • An intrinsic terminator is an inverted repeat (~20 bp) followed by a string of about 8 A–T base pairs. These elements act only once they are transcribed: they function in the RNA, not the DNA.
  • The inverted repeat lets the RNA fold into a stem-loop (hairpin). The hairpin causes termination by (i) pushing the polymerase forward, (ii) pulling the transcript out of the polymerase, or (iii) changing the polymerase's conformation. Mutations that disrupt the hairpin reduce or abolish termination.
  • The hairpin works only if it is followed by a run of U's. When it forms, the RNA is then held on the template by rU:dA pairs only, the weakest of all base pairs, so it comes away easily.
  • A Rho-dependent terminator has a rut site (rho utilisation): about 40 nt, C-rich, G-poor, with no hairpin.
  • Rho is a ring-shaped hexamer of 6 identical subunits with helicase and ATPase activity. It binds the rut site on the single-stranded RNA as it leaves the polymerase, then moves along the RNA using ATP until it reaches the polymerase. There it unwinds the RNA–DNA hybrid, the RNA is released, and Rho and the polymerase dissociate.

Chapter 7

Inhibitors of transcription

Two classic drugs act at different steps. Comparing them is a good test of how well you understand initiation and elongation.

Key points
  • Rifampicin (rifampin) binds a pocket in the β subunit (gene rpoB) next to the active site, in the path the RNA takes. It allows the first phosphodiester bonds to form but blocks the RNA from growing beyond 2–3 nt, so it inhibits initiation. Polymerases that have already escaped the promoter are not affected and finish their transcripts.
  • Rifampicin does not bind eukaryotic RNA polymerases, so it is a selective antibiotic, used especially against Mycobacterium tuberculosis. Resistance usually comes from point mutations in rpoB.
  • Actinomycin D intercalates into double-stranded DNA, mainly at G:C pairs, and blocks the movement of RNA polymerase, so it inhibits elongation. It works on DNA rather than on the enzyme, so it blocks transcription in both bacteria and eukaryotes. It is too toxic for use as an antibiotic but is used as an anticancer drug.
