🍫 The SweetCo Chocolate Company stamps a promise on every wrapper: “mean glucose: 100 mg per 100 g.” Regulators require that promise to be honest, and the brand’s reputation rides on it. Bars vary naturally from one to the next — glucose wanders with a standard deviation of σ = 1 mg/100g — so even a perfectly tuned machine produces batches whose averages wobble around the claim.

You are the quality inspector. Testing every bar is impossible, so you draw a batch of n bars, average their glucose (x̄), and must decide: does the batch pass, or do you reject the company’s claim? Two mistakes haunt you. Reject a good batch and you trigger a needless, expensive recall — a false alarm. Wave through a drifted machine and mislabeled bars reach customers — a costly miss. Your decision rule is fixed before you test: reject the claim whenever x̄ lands beyond the red lines, and choose α to cap how often you cry wolf on innocent batches.

Why does α equal the chance of a Type I error? — in plain words

You make a rule before testing. You say: “If the batch average falls outside these red lines, I will reject the company’s claim.” You fix this rule before you see any numbers, and you do not change it afterwards.
α is just the size of the red zone. You place the red lines so the red area is exactly α. Pick α = 0.05 and the red zone covers exactly 5% of the curve — the curve of a good machine. Nothing more is hidden in α. It is a size.
A Type I error = good machine + unlucky batch. Even a perfect machine produces an odd batch once in a while, just by luck. If that unlucky batch lands in the red zone, you reject a claim that was actually true. That mistake is a Type I error.
So the chance of that mistake is just the red area. If 5% of all good batches land in the red zone, then 5% of the time you will reject a good batch by mistake. That is the whole reason why α = the chance of a Type I error.
Level of significance (α) = chance of a Type I error
= chance of rejecting the claim when the claim is actually true
Read the formula like a sentence: “The claim is true. Even then, how often will my test reject it? Answer: α of the time.” When the machine has drifted, the mistake has a different name — β — and a different chance.
  • α does not tell you how likely the claim is true. It says nothing about the claim itself. You pick α yourself, before testing — it is the size of your red zone, nothing else.
  • α is not “the chance of being wrong” in general. It counts only one kind of mistake: rejecting a true claim. The other mistake — letting a drifted machine pass — has its own chance, called β.
  • Smaller α is not always better. Shrink the red zone and you cry wolf less often — but you also miss more real drifts. Fewer false alarms, more misses. It is a seesaw, and you choose where to sit.
