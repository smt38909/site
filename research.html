Research & Publications

Research overview

Bacterial synthesis of metal nanoparticles

Environmental bacteria — Acinetobacter species in particular — can be induced to reduce metal salts into nanoparticles of controlled size and shape. My doctoral and subsequent work established the conditions under which this happens, identified lignin peroxidase and a purified protein fraction as agents of the reduction, and characterised the silver, gold and selenium particles that result. The appeal is that the synthesis runs in water, at room temperature, without the reducing agents that conventional routes depend on.

What the particles are good for

Biogenic nanoparticles turn out to be active where conventional antimicrobials are struggling. We have shown silver nanoparticles disrupting Acinetobacter biofilms, acting in synergy with existing antibiotics against nosocomial strains, working against Candida albicans, and — with phytogenic silver and gold particles — against Mycobacterium tuberculosis. Selenium nanoparticles from Acinetobacter sp. SW30 proved cytotoxic to breast cancer cells.

Cleaning up: dyes, microplastics and waste as a resource

Textile dye in water is both a pollutant and a convenient test substrate. Recent work uses banana and carrot peel waste as a biosorbent for procion magenta and black azabache dyes, and gold nanocatalysts to decolorise dye effluent. A current review thread asks whether nano-adsorbents can be made to capture microplastics.

Science education

A parallel strand, with colleagues at SIES, looks at how teachers actually learn — the effect of online professional courses on teaching practice, experiential learning that blends MOOCs with project work and internships, and how public understanding of food-contact materials shifts with education.
